How it works

None of this is video. Every frame is drawn live, in your browser.

Two people, as Gaussian splats

The figures are a volumetric capture of two actors, held as 674,612 Gaussian splats: soft, coloured blobs that add up to a photograph you can walk around.

Lit after the fact

A splat has the light it was captured in baked into it, and no surface to shade. So a hidden mesh of the pair is built from the splats themselves and lit by ordinary lights. Every pixel of the splats is then multiplied by the light that falls on that mesh. That is what lets a torch light a photograph.

Shadows that are really cast

The torch is a spot light with a shadow map, held just beside the lens. A second, more exact mesh casts the shadows: an arm across a face, the two of them across the ground. Lightning is another light, from a new part of the sky each time.

Wet ground

The asphalt and its puddles are made in the shader. The reflection is a second, thinner copy of the splats standing upside down beneath the ground, lit to match and seen only where water lies. Rings from the rain bend it.

Rain and mist

4,500 drops fall, and each one's splash is worked out from where it has to land, with nothing simulated. Rain and mist are dark until the torch or the lightning finds them, which is why you can see the beam in the air. Splats have no depth of their own, so the mist looks up how far off the figures are, pixel by pixel, to pass behind them.

A little life

A capture is one frozen instant. Here the two of them breathe and sway by a few millimetres, and the wind moves her hair and the hem of her dress.

Sound to match

Thunder arrives late by the distance of the strike, and from its side of the sky. The rain is three recordings layered; the wind is synthesised, and follows the same gusts that move her hair.

In a browser

It runs on the PlayCanvas engine, on WebGPU where there is one and WebGL 2 where there is not. About 19 MB to download, and nothing to install.
